Does longshore drift occur at Whitstable?

Tankerton beach · groyne field · 10:00–12:00
  • H1: That longshore drift operates along Tankerton beach
  • H2: That sediment is moved from east to west
Why here: a managed shingle beach with a full groyne field, so the effect of drift is visible as a difference either side of each groyne.
PAPER 2 · HUMAN

To what extent is traffic a problem in Whitstable?

Harbour St · High St · Oxford St · 13:00–15:00
  • H1: That traffic problems are greatest nearest the harbour
  • H2: That residents see traffic as more of a problem than visitors
Why here: the B2205 runs through the town centre, and the Local Plan names congestion on the High Street and Oxford Street as the main issue.

The three streets we sampled

Site 1 Harbour Street. Narrowest, most pedestrians, fewest vehicles (37).
Site 2 High Street. Trial one-way system running. Best quality score (−1).
Site 3 Oxford Street. Widest, carries the B2205, most vehicles (71).

Physical: what we did and what we found

Longshore drift float survey

A float timed over 10 m at the waterline, three attempts, at 10:25.
Found: mean 54.7 seconds, travelling west every time. Speed 0.18 m/s.
Evaluate: wind pushes the float as well as the water, so this measures both.

Groyne profile

Drop from the top of the groyne down to the pebbles, both sides, every 4 m.
Found: the drop was smaller on the EAST side at every point. Mean west 0.70 m, east 0.49 m, difference 0.21 m. The difference peaked at 0.36 m around the middle of the groyne.
Evaluate: 0 m was the water's edge, which moved as the tide rose all morning.

Beach profile and sediment

Angle measured seaward from the sea wall; ten pebbles sampled systematically at each distance.
Found: the east side was steeper (up to 11°). Pebbles west of the groyne were smaller (37.5 mm v 52.3 mm) and rounder (4.1 v 3.0).
A Tankerton groyne. More shingle banked against the far side is drift made visible.
Groyne 3: the west drop is greater at every distance. That gap is the evidence.
All five groynes. Groyne 5 is the anomaly at just 0.05 m.

Physical conclusion

Longshore drift does occur at Whitstable and it moves shingle from east to west. Two separate lines of evidence agree: the drop to the pebbles was smaller on the east side of every groyne (mean difference 0.21 m), and pebbles west of the groyne were both smaller and rounder, which is what transport and attrition do to sediment. Both hypotheses are accepted. Confidence is high but not total, because Groyne 5 showed almost no difference at 0.05 m, probably because it sits closest to the harbour arm, which cuts off the supply of material from the east.

Physical evaluation

The groyne profile was repeatable and gave a clear pattern, but 0 m was defined as the water's edge, which moved inland as the tide rose, so groups measuring later did not start in the same place. We sampled one morning on one tide, so the result is a snapshot rather than a seasonal picture. The float survey measured wind as well as water. Next time: fix the datum to the seaward tip of the groyne, which does not move, and run the float three times at each of three points along the beach.

Human: what we did and what we found

Traffic count

Three counts of two minutes at each site, tallied by vehicle type, rotated so every site was counted at all three times.
Found: mean traffic rose inland along the B2205: 37 at Harbour Street, 53 at High Street, 71 at Oxford Street. Heavy vehicles rose from 2 to 11.
Evaluate: two minutes is short. One bus pulling out distorts a whole count.

Environmental Quality Survey

Eight criteria scored on a bipolar scale from −2 to +2 at each site.
Found: Harbour Street −7, High Street −1, Oxford Street −8. Harbour Street had the fewest vehicles but nearly the worst score.
Evaluate: the scores are our own opinion. Two people scoring independently would reduce the bias.

Noise survey and questionnaire

Decibel readings plus what we could hear; 25 questionnaires pooled from five groups.
Found: noise 68, 65 and 73 dBA. 12 of the 14 residents said traffic is a problem, but only 3 of the 11 visitors did.
Evaluate: 15 of the 25 responses came from the High Street, so the sample is not spread evenly.
Traffic by vehicle type. The whole bar rises inland; so does the heavy-vehicle band.
All three scores sit below zero. Harbour Street is nearly as bad as Oxford Street.
Criterion by criterion. High Street's shape sits furthest out, so it scores best.

Human conclusion

Traffic is a problem in Whitstable, but only in some places and for some people, which is what "to what extent" is asking. It is worst on Oxford Street, where we counted a mean of 71 vehicles in two minutes and the quality score was −8, the lowest of the three. Harbour Street breaks the simple pattern: it had the fewest vehicles at 37 yet scored −7, because it is the narrowest street and carries the heaviest pedestrian flow, so conflict matters more there than volume. The High Street scored only −1, helped by the trial one-way system running during our visit, which shows the problem is not fixed everywhere and can be reduced. Hypothesis 1 is rejected, because problems were greatest furthest from the harbour, not nearest it. Hypothesis 2 is accepted: 12 of 14 residents said traffic is a problem against only 3 of 11 visitors.

Human evaluation

Three of our five groups ran out of time and sampled three points along the High Street instead of all three streets, so Oxford Street rests on fewer counts and 15 of the 25 questionnaires came from one street. Each traffic count lasted only two minutes and we sampled a single afternoon out of season, when a seaside town is quietest. The quality survey was our own judgement rather than a measurement. Next time: count for five minutes at three times of day, fix the group rota before leaving, and have two people score the quality survey independently and take the mean, which would make the comparison between sites far more valid.

1. Learn it

Global atmospheric circulation

The Sun heats the Equator most strongly. Air moves in three loops, or cells, in each hemisphere: the Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells. Where air rises you get rain; where it sinks you get dry weather.

LatitudeAirPressureResult
Equator (0°)Hot air rises, cools and condensesLowHeavy rain: tropical rainforests
30° N and SAir sinks and warms, so no condensationHighClear skies, little rain: hot deserts
60° N and SAir rises where cells meetLowWet and changeable: the UK
Poles (90°)Cold, dry air sinksHighCold and dry
The rule to remember: rising air means low pressure, clouds and rain. Sinking air means high pressure and drought. Everything about El Niño follows from this.

El Niño and La Niña: how they lead to drought

Normal yearEl NiñoLa Niña
Trade windsBlow east to westWeaken or reverseStronger than normal
Warm waterPushed west, piles up near Australia and IndonesiaFlows back east towards South AmericaEven more piled up in the west
AustraliaWarm sea, rising air, rainAir sinks: high pressure and droughtHeavy rain and flooding
Peru and Chile coastCold water wells up, air sinks, dryWarm water, rising air, heavy rain and floodsColder sea, sinking air: drought
The point most students miss: drought is caused by sinking air. In an El Niño year the rising air and rain move east across the Pacific, so the air over Australia sinks, clouds cannot form and the rain fails. La Niña does the same thing to the west coast of South America.

Don't mix up these two exam maps

Watch out: the drought map shows the highest risk in a strip down the coast of Peru and Chile. That coast is normally dry because of the cold sea and sinking air. In an El Niño year it gets rain and floods, not drought. If you write "El Niño causes drought in Peru", you have the two maps the wrong way round.

Plates: what moves them

The crust and the top of the mantle form the rigid lithosphere, broken into plates. Below it is the outer core (liquid) and the inner core (solid). Plates are moved mainly by two forces:

  • Slab pull: at a trench, old oceanic plate is cold and dense, so it sinks into the mantle. As it sinks it pulls the rest of the plate behind it. This is the strongest force.
  • Ridge push: at a mid-ocean ridge, new plate is hot and sits high. It slides down and away from the ridge under gravity, pushing the plate outwards.

The four plate boundaries

How an earthquake happens: three steps

  1. Movement: slab pull and ridge push move the plates towards, apart from or past each other.
  2. Sticking: the plates lock together. Friction stops them moving, so pressure and energy build up.
  3. Release: the pressure becomes too great, the plates suddenly jolt and the energy is released as seismic waves.

2. Case study: the Kashmir earthquake, 2005

Date8 October 2005, 08:50 local time
Magnitude7.6
Focus26 km deep
Epicentre19 km (12 miles) north-east of Muzaffarabad, Pakistan-administered Kashmir

Causes

  • Kashmir sits on the collision boundary where the Indian Plate pushes north into the Eurasian Plate.
  • Both plates are continental and the same density, so neither subducts. The crust folds upwards, forming the Himalayas.
  • The earthquake happened on a reverse fault: one block of crust jolted vertically upwards, releasing a huge amount of energy.
  • The shallow focus (26 km) meant the shaking at the surface was very strong.
  • There are no volcanoes here: the crust is so thick that magma cools and solidifies before it can erupt.
  • Kashmir is steep and mountainous, so the shaking set off landslides.

Consequences

Primary: caused directly by the shakingSecondary: followed in the days, weeks and months after
More than 80,000 deaths, about a third from landslidesContaminated water spread water-borne disease
About 100,000 injuredA very cold winter followed: people in tents died of hypothermia
Homes, schools and roads collapsed across about 40,000 km²About 3.5 million homeless
Water pipes and power lines brokenSchools closed for an average of 14 months
Landslides buried whole villages and blocked roadsAbout US$4 billion of damage, slowing the economy for years

Responses

Immediate: hours and daysLong-term: months and years
International rescue teams, helicopters and sniffer dogs reached remote villages cut off by landslides40,000 people relocated to a new settlement
The Red Cross gave out tents, blankets and medical suppliesGovernment and aid money rebuilt homes, schools, roads and infrastructure
Judging success: helicopters were vital because landslides had blocked the roads, but the mountains, the cold and the sheer number of homeless people meant many could not be reached before winter.

Mitigation: reducing the impact next time

  • Prediction: scientists can say where earthquakes are likely, but not when.
  • Protection: earthquake-resistant buildings that sway rather than collapse.
  • Preparation: drills, emergency supplies and early warning systems.

Weathering and mass movement

Weathering breaks rock down where it is. Mass movement moves the loosened material downhill under gravity.

  • Chemical weathering: rainwater is slightly acidic and slowly dissolves rocks like limestone.
  • Biological weathering: plant roots grow into cracks and split rock apart.

Erosion, transport and deposition

Erosion processWhat happens
Hydraulic actionThe force of the water hits the bed and banks, forcing air into cracks and breaking rock apart.
AbrasionStones carried by the river scrape and wear away the bed and banks, like sandpaper.
AttritionStones knock into each other and become smaller and rounder. This erodes the load, not the channel.
SolutionThe water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone.
Exam trap: because attrition only wears down the stones, the 2022 mark scheme gave no credit for attrition when explaining how a V-shaped valley forms.
Transport processWhat happens
TractionLarge boulders roll along the bed.
SaltationSmall pebbles and sand bounce along the bed.
SuspensionFine material such as silt and clay is carried in the water.
SolutionDissolved minerals are carried in the water.

Deposition happens when the river loses energy and drops its load: on the inside of bends, when a flood spreads over the floodplain, and at the mouth.

Landforms: learn each one as a chain

V-shaped valley and interlocking spurs

  1. In the upper course the river has a steep gradient, so it erodes vertically (downwards) by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  2. The channel deepens and the valley sides become steep and unstable.
  3. Weathering loosens rock on the valley sides.
  4. Mass movement carries the loose material down the slope into the river, which carries it away. The valley becomes a V shape.
  5. The river winds around harder rock it cannot erode, leaving interlocking spurs.

Waterfall and gorge

  1. A band of hard rock lies over softer rock.
  2. The softer rock erodes faster by hydraulic action and abrasion, so the hard rock is undercut, leaving an overhang.
  3. Falling water and swirling rocks erode a deep plunge pool.
  4. The overhang is no longer supported and collapses into the plunge pool.
  5. This repeats, so the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.

Meanders and ox-bow lakes

  1. Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend, so lateral erosion cuts into the bank, forming a steep river cliff.
  2. Water flows slowest on the inside, so the river deposits sand and shingle, forming a gentle slip-off slope.
  3. Erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside make the bend grow and migrate.
  1. Erosion on the outside of two bends narrows the neck of land between them.
  2. During a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shortest, straighter route.
  3. Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an ox-bow lake.

Floodplain and levees

  1. When the river floods, it overtops its banks and spreads out.
  2. The water slows down and loses energy, so it deposits its load.
  3. The largest, heaviest material is dropped first, right at the edge of the channel. Finer silt is carried further across the floodplain.
  4. After many floods, the coarse material builds up into raised banks called levees, and the layers of silt build a flat, fertile floodplain.

2. Case study: the River Eden basin

● SalkeldSource

Location

North-west England, beside the Lake District. The source is in south Cumbria. The river flows north-west past Appleby, Penrith and Carlisle, reaching the sea at the Solway Firth on the border with Scotland.

Climate

Cumbria is on the west coast, so it is mild and wet. It is one of the wettest parts of the UK, with periods of intense rain. Near the source it is colder at altitude, so freeze-thaw weathering is common. In heavy rain the ground becomes saturated, the river rises quickly and flooding is more likely.

Geology

  • Hard, resistant rock such as gritstone on the high ground around the edge of the basin, and limestone, which is weathered chemically by rain.
  • Impermeable igneous rock in the west causes lots of surface run-off, so there is strong vertical erosion and steep V-shaped valleys in the upper course.
  • The middle and lower course is mostly softer sandstone, so lateral erosion widens the valley.

Landforms

  • Hell Gill Force: a waterfall close to the source.
  • Salkeld: large meanders; an ox-bow lake where Briggle Beck joins the Eden.
  • Carlisle: a city built on the Eden's floodplain, less than 100 m above sea level, so it floods often.

Management

SchemeTypeFactsHow successful?
Re-meandering Swindale BeckSoftStraightened in the 1850s to reduce flooding, which made the water flow faster and caused more flooding. Meanders put back in 2016 for about £200,000.Slows the flow and restores habitat, but straightening shows management can make things worse.
Tree plantingSoft10,000 trees planted around Ullswater in 2018; 1,000 near Dalston.Trees intercept rain, so less reaches the river. Cheap (volunteers planted them) and good for wildlife, but takes years to work.
Carlisle flood defencesHardAfter the January 2005 flood (about 1,800 properties flooded, 3 deaths), a £38m scheme of flood walls and embankments was built on the Eden, Petteril and Caldew to resist a 1-in-200-year flood.The Environment Agency says it prevented over £180m of damage in 2012 and 2013. But see Storm Desmond below.
Storm Desmond, December 2015: a UK record 341.4 mm of rain in 24 hours fell at Honister Pass. The Eden reached 7.8 m, higher than the 7.2 m of 2005. The defences were overtopped and about 1,900 homes and businesses flooded. Since then, over 6 km of new or raised defences have been built, protecting about 1,650 more homes.
Your judgement: the hard defences worked against the floods they were designed for, then were beaten by a bigger one. Soft engineering upstream, like tree planting and re-meandering, slows water before it reaches Carlisle. A strong answer argues that the Eden needs both.

A plan that reaches Level 3: paragraph 1, Carlisle's £38m defences and how they protected the city until 2015. Paragraph 2, Storm Desmond overtopping them. Paragraph 3, soft engineering upstream: Swindale Beck and tree planting. Conclusion: to what extent, and why both types are needed.

1. Learn it

Megacities and world cities

A megacity is a city with 10 million people or more. The UN counted 8 megacities in 1975 and 33 in 2025, and 19 of them are in Asia. Jakarta is now the biggest, with nearly 42 million people, followed by Dhaka and Tokyo. By 2050 there should be 37, as cities such as Addis Ababa and Dar es Salaam pass 10 million.

Is London a megacity? Greater London, the 32 boroughs and the City, had about 9.1 million people in mid-2025 (ONS), so by the usual definition it is not. The UN's 2025 report measures the whole built-up area around a city, which is bigger, and that is why London appears on this map. In the exam, use 9.1 million.

A world city has influence far beyond its own country: headquarters of big companies (TNCs), a global financial centre, an international airport hub, and a large, diverse migrant population. London is a world city even though it is not a megacity.

How cities grow and change

ProcessWhat it meansMost typical of

Re-urbanisationPeople move back into inner-city areas, often after regenerationACs, for example Peckham

Rapid urban growth in LIDCs: the consequences

  • Housing cannot be built fast enough, so informal settlements (slums) grow.
  • Not enough clean water or sanitation, so diseases spread.
  • Too few formal jobs, so many people work in the informal sector.
  • Transport cannot cope, so congestion gets worse; schools and hospitals are overstretched.

London also has over 40 universities and about 400,000 students, including a third of the UK's international students, and is a world centre for culture, museums, shopping and theatre in the West End.

Migration and ways of life

National and international migration has made London the most ethnically diverse region of the UK, and made its population young, because most migrants are young adults. Diversity varies a lot across the city.

● Newham● Richmond

In Newham, 69.2% of people are non-white; in Richmond upon Thames, 19.5%.

Challenges: inequality, transport and air pollution

  • Congestion: drivers lost an average of 99 hours in traffic in 2023, more than in any other European city.
  • Air pollution: about 4,000 deaths a year in London are linked to poor air quality. In 2013, nine-year-old Ella Kissi-Debrah from south-east London died after an asthma attack; she was the first person in the UK to have air pollution recorded as a cause of death.
  • Deprived areas suffer most: they are more likely to be next to busy roads.

1. Learn it

Measuring development

Development means an improvement in people's standard of living and quality of life. No single number captures it, so geographers combine several.

MeasureWhat it shows
GNI per capitaAverage income per person: economic development
Life expectancyHow long people live: health care, diet, clean water
Literacy rateThe share of adults who can read and write: education
HDI (Human Development Index)Combines life expectancy, education and income into one score from 0 to 1

The IMF groups countries as ACs (advanced countries), EDCs (emerging and developing countries) and LIDCs (low-income developing countries). Most LIDCs are in Africa. The difference between the richest and poorest countries is the development gap.

Why development is uneven

Physical factorsHuman factors
Landlocked: no coastline makes trade harder and more expensiveColonialism: resources and wealth were taken by the colonising country
Climate: drought or extreme heat makes farming difficultPolitics: conflict, corruption and unstable government stop investment
Natural hazards: rebuilding after disasters costs money and can lead to debtDebt: money spent repaying loans cannot be spent on schools or health care
Natural resources: oil or minerals can earn money, but only if the profits stay in the countryTrade: relying on raw materials leaves a country exposed when prices fall

Top-down development is decided by governments or big organisations such as the World Bank. Bottom-up development is when NGOs work with local communities to find out what they need.

Rostow's model of development

In 1960 the economist Walt Rostow argued that every country passes through the same five stages as it develops. Your lesson places the DRC at stage 2.

1Traditional society
2Preconditions for take-offThe DRC
3Take-off
4Drive to maturity
5Age of high mass consumption
StageWhat it looks likeExample
1 Traditional societySubsistence farming, little trade or technologyNo countries today
2 Preconditions for take-offMost people farm; investment in roads, mining and the first industries beginsDRC, Central African Republic

Criticism: Rostow based the model on European countries. It ignores colonialism, conflict and debt, which is exactly what has held the DRC back.

2. Case study: the Democratic Republic of the Congo

Location: a huge country in central Africa, crossed by the Equator, with only a tiny strip of coast on the Atlantic. Most of the north is tropical rainforest. It is rich in minerals such as cobalt and copper.

History: how the DRC's development was held back

  1. 1885: King Leopold II of Belgium seized the Congo as his personal property, the Congo Free State. People were forced to collect rubber and ivory. Millions died; some historians estimate the population fell by as many as 10 million.
  2. 1908: international protest forced Leopold to hand the Congo to Belgium. Wealth kept flowing out to Europe.
  3. 30 June 1960: independence. Joseph Kasa-Vubu became the first President and Patrice Lumumba the first Prime Minister. With little preparation, a power vacuum led to five years of conflict.
  4. 1965: Mobutu Sese Seko seized power and ruled until 1997. Corruption was huge: by 1970 he was estimated to have stolen 60% of the national budget, while roads crumbled and public workers went unpaid.
  5. 1989: the DRC stopped repaying its loans, so development programmes were cancelled.

Global connections: cobalt

The DRC produces over 70% of the world's cobalt, used in rechargeable batteries for phones and electric cars. Metals make up 92% of its exports.

Blessing or curse? Cobalt brings jobs and about $2 billion in exports, but relying on one product makes the economy unstable when prices fall, the work is dangerous, and most profits go abroad.

Progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals

In 2015 the UN replaced the 8 Millennium Development Goals you studied with 17 Sustainable Development Goals, to be met by 2030. The exam asks about progress towards three. Learn these:

SDGProgress in the DRCJudgement
1: No povertyAbout 74% of people live on less than $2.15 a day (World Bank, 2023). The share has fallen slightly over the last decade, but because the population is growing so fast, the number of poor people has risen.Very limited progress
3: Good healthDeaths of children under five fell from about 180 per 1,000 births in 1990 to about 90 in 2024 (UNICEF). But that is still about 385,000 children a year, and the 2030 target is 25 or fewer.Real progress, but far from the target
